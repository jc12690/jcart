Propulsion Systems Laboratory Virtual Simulator: 450-lb Airline Heater Model

NASA Glenn Research Center · 2021 · Propulsion Systems Analyst

A virtual simulator for the Propulsion Systems Laboratory (PSL) at Glenn Research Center has been in development for several years to provide operators experience with conducting test runs prior to stepping into the actual control room. This project focused on the addition of a heater on the 450-lb airline in the simulator for the PSL. Mass flow and temperature relations were explored to generate three equations that were then tested against actual data from a PSL test run to determine one that best represented the actual data. This formula was then adapted into code that enabled the simulator to emulate the addition of fuel into the heater to heat the air on the journey to its designated test cell. Upon completion, the simulator was able to run smoothly and with values that mirrored those in the test run conducted in 2009.

Threat Intelligence Investigation

CHEQ 2026 · Interview Take-Home
Threat IntelligenceBot DetectionAd FraudE-Commerce Security

A take-home assessment for CHEQ's interview process, run on real data: I connected to CHEQ's servers and used Python and SQL to analyze 126,959 e-commerce sessions for bot traffic and ad fraud, quantify the financial impact, and build a prioritized mitigation strategy to protect checkout, login, and advertising spend.

The Problem

E-commerce platforms are a high-value target for automated abuse, and most of it is invisible at first glance. Bots mimicking legitimate user behavior inflate traffic metrics, drain advertising budgets, hoard inventory, and stuff credential databases, all while flying under the radar of conventional monitoring. CHEQ's take-home handed me real session data and a question that's harder than it sounds: which of these 126,959 sessions are actually human?

What’s Included

  • Python and SQL against CHEQ's servers to pull and query the session data
  • Behavioral signal analysis across 126,959 e-commerce sessions to identify automated bot traffic
  • Multi-signal correlation including datacenter ASNs, headless browser indicators, timezone mismatches, and interaction speed analysis
  • Bot-driven attack pattern detection targeting checkout, login, and product endpoints
  • Ad fraud quantification across paid Google and Bing search traffic consumed by automated sessions
  • Prioritized mitigation strategy covering ASN blocking, device fingerprint detection, rate limiting, and MFA enforcement

Impact

Identified that 25.51% of all sessions, roughly 1 in 4, were automated bot traffic. The analysis estimated $3,362 to $13,448 in advertising spend wasted on bots interacting with paid search campaigns, and surfaced attack patterns consistent with two of the most revenue-critical threats on any e-commerce platform: inventory hoarding and credential stuffing at the checkout and login layers. I wrote it up as a presentation for the interview.

Founder

Retrac Labs 2025–Present
PrivacyThreat ModelingSwiftApp Store

Retrac Labs is my independent studio for privacy-first Mac and iPhone apps. Three are on the App Store, and each keeps what you give it on your own device: no accounts, no cloud, and on the Mac, no way for the app to reach the network at all.

Why

Most apps that handle sensitive data, from voice recordings to clipboard history to cycle logs, send a copy somewhere: a server, an analytics vendor, a cloud backup. Every copy is another place it can leak, be sold, or be handed over. Cycle logs show the stakes most plainly, since in the US, health data held by an app company can be subpoenaed.

Retrac Labs starts from the opposite assumption: the safest copy of your data is the only one, on your own device, and a privacy promise should be something you can check rather than take on faith.

What Shipped

  • Apunte for Mac: transcription of recordings, calls, and meetings, run entirely on the Mac, with on-device summaries and a Touch ID lock
  • Retazo for Mac: clipboard history that skips password-manager copies, lets one-time codes fade after five minutes, and strips tracking tags from links
  • Amparo for iPhone: cycle tracking with no account or cloud, kept out of iCloud Backup by default, with Discreet Mode and an encrypted AirDrop transfer to a new phone
  • Mac apps shipped without the outgoing-network entitlement, so their own code cannot open a connection, which anyone can confirm with codesign
  • Product, design, engineering, and App Store releases, from first commit to shipped update

Impact

Three apps are live on the App Store as of October 2026, and each has already shipped several updates. Every privacy claim is built to be checked rather than trusted: one codesign command shows the Mac apps can't reach the network, and Amparo's data stays out of the cloud unless the user chooses otherwise. It's security work aimed at the user's side of the threat model: decide what could be taken, then make sure it never leaves the device.

Check It Yourself

Every Mac app declares what it's allowed to do, and Apple signs that declaration into the app. Anyone can read it. Here's Apunte 1.3:

$ codesign -d --entitlements - --xml /Applications/Apunte.app | plutil -p -
{
  "com.apple.application-identifier" => "2JC8Y26788.com.retraclabs.apunte"
  "com.apple.developer.team-identifier" => "2JC8Y26788"
  "com.apple.security.app-sandbox" => true
  "com.apple.security.device.audio-input" => true
  "com.apple.security.files.user-selected.read-only" => true
  "com.apple.security.files.user-selected.read-write" => true
}

Apunte asks for the sandbox, the microphone, and files you choose. Retazo 2.1 asks for the sandbox and files you choose. Neither carries com.apple.security.network.client, the entitlement a sandboxed Mac app needs to open a network connection, so neither can send what it holds anywhere.

Amparo's Threat Model

Cycle logs are the most sensitive data any of these apps holds, so Amparo is designed around who might want them:

  • A company or server holding your data, through a breach, a sale, or a subpoena: Amparo has no account and no server, and its code makes no network requests of its own, so there's no copy to hand over. Its data is kept out of iCloud Backup by default.
  • Someone glancing at your phone: Discreet Mode covers notifications and the Lock Screen, alternate app icons don't look like a cycle tracker, and Siri keeps quiet about your cycle while the iPhone is locked.
  • The move to a new iPhone, intercepted or AirDropped to the wrong person: the transfer file is sealed with AES-GCM under a key derived from a 12-character one-time code (60 bits) through 600,000 rounds of PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256. The code appears only on the sending iPhone, and the file's header is bound into the seal, so an altered file won't open.
  • History piling up: Keep My History deletes entries older than a period you choose.

Out of scope: someone holding your unlocked iPhone, or a compromised device. And if you turn iCloud Backup on for Amparo, your data is then protected by Apple's backup, not by Amparo.
